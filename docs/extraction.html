How to extract

How each resource could be taken out of the Moon

Water, helium-3, oxygen and metals are locked in Moon soil in different ways, so each needs a different method. Here are the main ideas, how they work, and how far each has gotten.

0

No resource has been mined on the Moon yet. Everything below is a proposal or a test, mostly done on Earth with fake Moon soil (called "simulant") in vacuum chambers. The first drill sent to look for ice at the south pole (NASA's PRIME-1, 2025) landed on its side and could not dig, though its instruments worked. Which methods work best will depend on what future landers find, so these are different ways it could be done, not settled answers.

Water ice

Getting water out of the dark craters

Common question

Is it ice or water in the craters?

  • It is ice, not liquid water. NASA says Moon water is "mainly in the form of ice and not pools of liquid water."
  • It is mixed into the soil. It is not an ice sheet or a glacier. It is likely tiny ice grains mixed into the dirt, held at about −233 °C (40 kelvin) in craters that never see sunlight.
  • It may also be buried or form layers. NASA notes the ice "may be mixed in with the lunar soil, buried deep below the surface, or it could be a sheet of ice." Nobody has dug into it yet.
  • It never melts into liquid. In the Moon's vacuum, heated ice turns straight into vapor (sublimation). NASA's lab tests showed water coming off frozen soil well below its normal melting point.
  • The sunny Moon has a little too. NASA's SOFIA found water molecules in sunlit Clavius crater at 100–412 parts per million, about one 12-ounce bottle spread through a cubic meter of soil.

Three main ways it could be extracted

Lab-tested

1. Dig it up and bake it

  1. A rover or excavator scoops icy soil.
  2. The soil goes into a heated chamber (an oven).
  3. The ice turns to vapor and refreezes on a very cold surface (a "cold trap"), where it is collected.

Trade-off: simple and well understood, but means digging in −233 °C ground, where ice-rich soil may be as hard as rock.

NASA-funded concept

2. Heat it where it lies ("thermal mining")

  1. Mirrors on the sunlit crater rim bounce sunlight down into the dark crater.
  2. A clear tent over the ground traps the water vapor that rises.
  3. The vapor refreezes in cold-trap tanks, which robots haul to a plant.

Trade-off: no digging, but needs large mirrors and moving the tent from spot to spot. Proposed by the Colorado School of Mines with NASA funding.

Lab-tested

3. Microwave it

  1. A microwave emitter points at the ground, like a kitchen microwave aimed at the soil.
  2. The microwaves heat the soil from the inside, so it doesn't have to be dug up.
  3. The vapor is caught in a cold trap.

Result: in a NASA test on frozen fake Moon soil in a vacuum, 85% of the water came out in 5 minutes, and 99% of that was caught.

Then: turning ice into rocket fuel and air

Biggest worry: cleaning the water. Moon ice comes mixed with other frozen chemicals: NASA's LCROSS found carbon-, nitrogen- and sulfur-bearing ices along with the water. Impurities could make the fuel unusable or even unstable. Researchers can't fully test filters without real samples.
Helium-3

Shaking a rare gas loose from huge amounts of soil

Helium-3 is stuck in the outer skin of soil grains at a few parts per billion, so getting 1 kg means handling 100,000 to 1 million tonnes of soil. Two companies have published different plans.

Company plan · tests on Earth

Interlune: dig, sort, extract, separate

  1. Excavate: machines built with Vermeer scoop soil, about 100 tonnes an hour for the prototype.
  2. Sort: keep the finest grains, which hold the most helium-3; throw the coarse ones back.
  3. Extract: release the trapped gases. Interlune says its method uses ten times less power than heating; the classic method is heating to about 700 °C.
  4. Separate: chill the gas mix to extreme cold (cryogenic distillation) to pull helium-3 out from helium-4 and hydrogen.

Status: parts tested on Earth and on a reduced-gravity flight. A small test on the Moon is planned for 2028.

Company plan

Magna Petra: till the top few centimeters

  1. A device pulled by a rover shakes only the top few centimeters of soil, "like a tractor pulling a till."
  2. Loosely held helium-3 escapes and is captured in pressurized tanks.
  3. Heavier gases are vented off, leaving the lighter helium-3 to ship back to Earth.

Trade-off: much lighter equipment and almost no digging, but it only gets the loosely held helium-3, so yields are lower.

How the helium-3 got there

Oxygen, iron, titanium, aluminum & silicon

Splitting rock into air and metal

Moon rock is 40–45% oxygen, but the oxygen is chemically locked to metals. Pulling it free gives breathable air or rocket fuel, and leaves metals behind. These methods get both at once.

Field-tested on Earth

Hydrogen + ilmenite

  1. Separate the iron-titanium mineral ilmenite from the soil.
  2. Heat it with hydrogen gas to 700–1,000 °C. Out come iron, titanium dioxide and water.
  3. Split the water into oxygen (kept) and hydrogen (reused).

Result: a NASA test reactor on Hawaii's Mauna Kea volcano made 660 kg of oxygen in a year from volcanic soil.

Lab-tested · company scale-up

Melt it and run electricity through it

  1. Heat crushed soil to about 1,600 °C until it melts.
  2. Pass an electric current through the melt (molten regolith electrolysis).
  3. Oxygen bubbles off one side; iron, silicon and aluminum collect at the other.

Status: NASA finished a reactor test in 2025. Blue Origin's Blue Alchemist aims to turn the metals into solar cells and wire.

Lab-tested

Molten salt (soil stays solid)

  1. Put powdered soil in a basket inside molten calcium-chloride salt at 950 °C. The soil does not melt.
  2. Electricity pulls the oxygen out.
  3. What's left is a mixed metal alloy.

Result: ESA got "virtually all the oxygen" out of fake Moon soil.

Lab-tested

Concentrated sunlight + carbon

  1. Mirrors focus sunlight to heat soil inside a reactor (carbothermal reduction).
  2. Carbon pulls oxygen out as carbon monoxide.
  3. The process then releases oxygen that can be collected.

Status: NASA and Sierra Space confirmed oxygen production from fake Moon soil using sunlight in 2026.

Rare earths & thorium

No method yet, and first, a deposit has to be found

No Moon method designed

Why it's the hardest

  1. Rare earths sit in tiny phosphate minerals (mostly merrillite and apatite) inside KREEP rock.
  2. Merrillite makes up at most about 3% of the rock, which researchers say is too little to mine economically "based on current understanding."
  3. Orbit maps show only broad averages. The USGS says a few smaller deposits might be as rich as ores mined on Earth, but they have to be found first.

What would come first: close-up prospecting in the Imbrium region with landers and rovers, before anyone designs a processing method.

Platinum-group metals

Searching old impact craters

Only an idea so far

Find asteroid leftovers, then dig

  1. Up to about 6,500 craters (1 km or wider) might hold platinum-rich pieces of the asteroids that made them.
  2. A 2025 study says the best chance is near the edges of old "impact melt sheets," like the Sudbury nickel and platinum mines in Canada.
  3. No spacecraft has yet carried a sensor sharp enough to spot these metals from orbit.

Status: no deposit found. The Moon's own rock has far too little platinum to ever mine.
