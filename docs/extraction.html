How to extract

Mining the Moon, one resource at a time

Pick a resource below to see why it's worth extracting, how it could help people on Earth, and the different ways it could be pulled out of the Moon.

0Nothing has been mined on the Moon yet. The methods here are proposals and tests, mostly done on Earth with fake Moon soil in vacuum chambers. The first drill sent to hunt for ice at the south pole (NASA's PRIME-1, 2025) landed on its side and couldn't dig. These are ways it could be done, not settled answers.
01 · WATER ICE

Water ice

Frozen water hidden in craters at the poles that never see sunlight. It's the most useful resource on the Moon for people who live and travel there.

Why extract it

  • Water is three things in one: drinking water, oxygen to breathe, and rocket fuel. Electricity splits it into hydrogen and oxygen.
  • Less to launch from Earth. "The more material we can use on the Moon, the less we launch from Earth," as Lockheed Martin puts it.
  • A gas station in space. Fuel made at the Moon could refuel ships headed to Mars and beyond.
  • Big savings, if it works. An industry-led study found that refueling with Moon propellant could cut the cost of getting from Earth to the Moon's surface by more than three times.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • Possible futureCheaper space services. The same study names satellite companies as likely buyers of Moon fuel, which could lower the cost of launching the satellites people rely on.
  • TodayClues about the early solar system. The ice LCROSS found came with carbon, nitrogen and sulfur ices that point to comets. NASA says ice layers may hold long records of the Moon's environment.
  • Possible futureLess launching from Earth, "unburdening our environment at home," in Lockheed Martin's words.
The catchIt isn't proven to save money. A 2019 NASA-sponsored study found Moon-made propellant would cost 97% more than launching it from Earth, under its assumptions. Extraction systems would need to be 2–3 times more efficient to break even.
Common question

Is it ice or water in the craters?

  • Ice, not liquid water. NASA says Moon water is "mainly in the form of ice and not pools of liquid water."
  • Mixed into the soil. Not an ice sheet or a glacier: most likely tiny ice grains in the dirt, at about −233 °C (40 kelvin).
  • Maybe buried or in layers too. NASA says it "may be mixed in with the lunar soil, buried deep below the surface, or it could be a sheet of ice." Nobody has dug into it yet.
  • It never becomes liquid. In the Moon's vacuum, warmed ice turns straight into vapor (sublimation).
  • The sunny Moon has a trace too. NASA's SOFIA found about one 12-ounce bottle's worth per cubic meter of soil in sunlit Clavius crater.

How it could be extracted

Lab-tested

1. Dig it up and bake it

  1. A rover or excavator scoops icy soil.
  2. The soil goes into a heated chamber.
  3. The ice turns to vapor and refreezes on a very cold surface (a "cold trap").

Trade-off: simple, but means digging in −233 °C ground, where icy soil may be as hard as rock.

NASA-funded concept

2. Heat it where it lies

  1. Mirrors on the sunlit rim bounce sunlight into the dark crater.
  2. A clear tent over the ground traps the rising vapor.
  3. Cold-trap tanks refreeze it, and robots haul them to a plant.

Trade-off: no digging, but needs big mirrors and moving the tent. Proposed by the Colorado School of Mines with NASA funding ("thermal mining").

Lab-tested

3. Microwave it

  1. A microwave emitter points at the ground.
  2. The soil heats from the inside, with no digging.
  3. The vapor is caught in a cold trap.

Result: in a NASA test on frozen fake Moon soil in a vacuum, 85% of the water came out in 5 minutes, and 99% of that was caught.

Then: ice into rocket fuel and air

01CollectIce from the cold traps.
02CleanFreeze-distill and filter out dust and other frozen gases.
03SplitElectricity splits water into hydrogen and oxygen.
04ChillCool the gases into liquids.
05UseRocket fuel, breathing air, drinking water.
Biggest worryCleaning the water. Moon ice comes mixed with other frozen chemicals, and impurities could make the fuel unusable or unstable. Filters can't be fully tested without real samples.
02 · HELIUM-3

Helium-3

A rare, light form of helium the Sun has been depositing in Moon soil for billions of years. Earth has almost none.

Why extract it

  • Earth is running short. Earth's reserves are under 10 kg, and the main reliable source is the slow decay of tritium. Demand outran supply after 2001.
  • Buyers are already lined up. Bluefors agreed to buy up to 10,000 liters a year from 2028 to 2037, before any has been mined.
  • The Moon may hold over a million tonnes, though how much can really be collected is unknown.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • TodaySecurity: neutron detectors at borders and ports that catch smuggled nuclear material. About 80% of U.S. helium-3 use goes here.
  • TodayMedicine: special lung imaging. For some of these uses there is no known alternative.
  • TodayQuantum computers: the refrigerators that cool their chips to near absolute zero need helium-3.
  • Possible futureCleaner fusion power. Fusing deuterium with helium-3 releases a proton instead of a neutron, so the reactor would be less radioactive and could turn the energy straight into electricity. About 1% of the energy would still come out as neutrons.
The catchHelium-3 fusion needs far higher temperatures than today's fusion fuel and hasn't worked in a power plant. Collecting 1 kg means processing 100,000 to 1 million tonnes of soil.

How it could be extracted

Company plan · tests on Earth

Interlune: dig, sort, extract, separate

  1. Excavate: machines built with Vermeer scoop soil, about 100 tonnes an hour for the prototype.
  2. Sort: keep the finest grains, which hold the most helium-3.
  3. Extract: release the trapped gases. Interlune says its method uses ten times less power than heating; the classic method heats soil to about 700 °C.
  4. Separate: chill the gas to extreme cold to pull helium-3 apart from helium-4 and hydrogen.

Status: parts tested on Earth and on a reduced-gravity flight; a small Moon test is planned for 2028.

Company plan

Magna Petra: till the top few centimeters

  1. A device pulled by a rover shakes only the top few centimeters, "like a tractor pulling a till."
  2. Loosely held helium-3 escapes into pressurized tanks.
  3. Heavier gases are vented, leaving helium-3 to ship to Earth.

Trade-off: light equipment and almost no digging, but it only gets the loosely held helium-3, so yields are lower.

How the Sun put it there

03 · OXYGEN

Oxygen

The most common element in Moon soil, locked inside rock. Freeing it gives breathable air and half of rocket fuel.

Why extract it

  • It's everywhere: 40–45% of Moon soil by weight, so it can be made at any landing site, not just the poles.
  • Air and fuel: NASA studies describe oxygen plants as "refueling stations" for life support and propellant.
  • Cheaper landings: Blue Origin says making oxygen on the Moon could make landings up to 60% cheaper (company's own estimate).

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • TodayCleaner steel, born from Moon research. In the mid-2000s NASA paid MIT professor Donald Sadoway to find ways to make oxygen for Moon bases. Running electricity through iron-rich rock made oxygen, with metal left over. That idea became Boston Metal, which is developing steel made this way. Its only byproduct is oxygen, not carbon dioxide.
  • Possible futureCleaner steelmaking. Boston Metal aims to clean up the steel industry. It has built a prototype "green steel" reactor and already recovers valuable metals from mining waste in Brazil.
The catchEvery method needs very high temperatures (950–1,600 °C) and lots of power, which on the Moon means big solar arrays or nuclear power.

How it could be extracted

Field-tested on Earth

Hydrogen + ilmenite

  1. Separate the iron-titanium mineral ilmenite from the soil.
  2. Heat it with hydrogen to 700–1,000 °C. Out come iron, titanium dioxide and water.
  3. Split the water into oxygen (kept) and hydrogen (reused).

Result: a NASA test reactor on Hawaii's Mauna Kea volcano made 660 kg of oxygen in a year.

Lab-tested · company scale-up

Melt it, run electricity through it

  1. Melt crushed soil at about 1,600 °C.
  2. Pass a current through the melt (molten regolith electrolysis).
  3. Oxygen bubbles off; iron, silicon and aluminum collect at the other side.

Status: NASA finished a reactor test in 2025; Blue Origin's Blue Alchemist is scaling it up.

Lab-tested

Molten salt (soil stays solid)

  1. Put powdered soil in molten calcium-chloride salt at 950 °C; the soil doesn't melt.
  2. Electricity pulls the oxygen out.
  3. A mixed metal alloy is left behind.

Result: ESA got "virtually all the oxygen" out of fake Moon soil.

Lab-tested

Concentrated sunlight + carbon

  1. Mirrors focus sunlight to heat soil in a reactor.
  2. Carbon pulls oxygen out as carbon monoxide.
  3. The process then releases oxygen to collect.

Status: NASA and Sierra Space confirmed oxygen from fake Moon soil using sunlight in 2026.

04 · IRON

Iron

Plentiful in the dark lava plains. It comes out as a by-product when oxygen is made from rock.

Why extract it

  • Lots of it: lava-plain soil brought back by China's Chang'e-5 has 22.5% iron oxide.
  • Build on the Moon: Moon soil could become "construction materials for habitats and spacecraft" instead of launching heavy metal from Earth.
  • Comes for free: the hydrogen and melt-and-electricity oxygen methods leave iron behind.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • TodayGreen steel on Earth. Boston Metal's steelmaking method grew out of NASA-funded research on getting oxygen and metal from Moon rock. It makes iron with oxygen as the byproduct instead of carbon dioxide.
  • Possible futureLess launching: building with Moon iron means fewer heavy launches from Earth.
The catchRaw iron or mixed metal still has to be shaped into parts, which would need factories on the Moon.

How it could be extracted

Field-tested on Earth

Hydrogen + ilmenite

  1. Heat ilmenite with hydrogen at 700–1,000 °C.
  2. Iron metal is left behind, along with titanium dioxide and water.
Lab-tested

Melt + electricity

  1. Melt soil at about 1,600 °C and pass a current through it.
  2. Iron collects at one electrode, along with silicon and aluminum.
Lab-tested

Molten salt

  1. Remove the oxygen at 950 °C in molten salt.
  2. A mixed metal alloy remains.
05 · TITANIUM

Titanium

Held in the black mineral ilmenite. Moon lava is far richer in titanium than similar rock on Earth.

Why extract it

  • Rich deposits: up to about 12% titanium oxide at the richest spots, far more than similar rocks on Earth.
  • One mineral, three resources: ilmenite holds titanium and iron, gives up oxygen when heated with hydrogen, and holds helium-3 better than other minerals.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • No direct benefit yetNone of the sources describe a near-term benefit to Earth from Moon titanium. Its value is mainly for building and making oxygen on the Moon.
  • TodayScience: scientists still don't know why the Moon is so much richer in titanium than Earth. It suggests the Moon's interior had less oxygen when it formed, which helps explain how the Moon evolved.
The catchThe tested methods produce titanium dioxide or mixed metal alloys, not pure titanium metal.

How it could be extracted

Field-tested on Earth

Separate ilmenite, then heat with hydrogen

  1. Sort ilmenite out of the soil.
  2. Heat it with hydrogen; the titanium ends up as titanium dioxide, with iron and water.
Lab-tested

Molten salt

  1. Strip the oxygen from soil in molten salt at 950 °C.
  2. A mixed metal alloy is left.
06 · ALUMINUM & SILICON

Aluminum & silicon

Aluminum fills the bright highlands, and silicon is in almost every rock. Together they could make power systems on the Moon.

Why extract it

  • Plenty of both: highland samples hold 24–30% alumina, and silicon is one of the seven elements that make up 98–99% of Moon rock.
  • Make power on the Moon: silicon becomes solar cells, and aluminum becomes wire and cables.
  • Already demonstrated: Blue Origin's NASA-backed Blue Alchemist turns fake Moon soil into silicon solar cells and aluminum wire.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • Possible futureCleaner processing. Blue Origin says its method separates metals and silicon "without water, toxic chemicals, or carbon emissions" (company's own claim).
  • Possible futureLess launching: solar panels made on the Moon would not have to be launched from Earth.
The catchSolar cells need very pure silicon, and the process runs at about 1,600 °C.

How it could be extracted

Lab-tested · company scale-up

Melt + electricity (Blue Alchemist)

  1. Melt crushed soil at about 1,600 °C.
  2. A current separates metal and silicon from oxygen.
  3. Silicon is refined for solar cells; aluminum becomes wire.

Status: passed design review; a full autonomous ground demonstration was planned for 2026.

07 · RARE EARTHS & THORIUM

Rare earths

The magnet metals behind electric cars, wind turbines and defense systems. On the Moon they sit in rock called KREEP.

Why extract it

  • One country controls the supply. In 2024 China produced 60% of mined magnet rare earths, 91% of refined output and 94% of the magnets.
  • Supply is getting tighter. In 2025 China announced export controls on several rare earths.
  • Demand is growing, led by electric vehicles. They are also used in satellites and missile guidance.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • Possible futureA second supply. The USGS says a few Moon deposits might be as rich as ores mined on Earth. That could one day be a source outside today's concentrated supply chain.
  • Possible futureClean energy and cars: more supply for wind-turbine and electric-motor magnets.
The catchNo rich deposit has been found, and no Moon method has been designed. The rare-earth minerals make up at most about 3% of the rock, which researchers call too little to mine economically "based on current understanding."

How it could be extracted

No Moon method designed

Step one: find a deposit

  1. Orbit maps show only broad averages over huge areas.
  2. Landers and rovers would need to prospect the Imbrium region up close.
  3. Only then could a processing method be designed.
08 · PLATINUM-GROUP METALS

Platinum-group metals

Platinum, palladium and their relatives, possibly left in old craters by metal-rich asteroids.

Why extract it

  • Few sources on Earth: since 1900, about 90% of production has come from South Africa and Russia.
  • Needed for clean tech: catalytic converters use the most, and fuel cells and the electrolyzers that make clean hydrogen need platinum and iridium.

How it could help Earth & humanity

  • Possible futureMore supply for clean hydrogen, if a rich deposit is ever found and can be brought back.
  • TodayWorth knowing: on Earth, recycled catalytic converters, jewelry and electronics already supply enough to close the gap between mining and use.
The catchNo deposit has been found, and the Moon's own rock has far too little to ever mine. No spacecraft has carried a sensor sharp enough to find these metals.

How it could be extracted

Only an idea so far

Find asteroid leftovers, then dig

  1. Up to about 6,500 craters (1 km or wider) might hold platinum-rich asteroid remains.
  2. The best chance is near the edges of old impact melt sheets, like Canada's Sudbury mines.
  3. A new orbiting sensor would be needed to find them first.
