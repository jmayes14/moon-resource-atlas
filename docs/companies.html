What this means for supply security

Moon mining starts with Earth's supply chains

Before any helium-3 comes back, landers and digging machines need aluminum, copper, titanium and electronics from Germany, India, the United States and China.

Rides are the bottleneck

Since Apollo ended in 1972, only one U.S. spacecraft has landed intact: Firefly's Blue Ghost in 2025. Every U.S. lander planned for 2026 has now slipped into 2027.

Quantum computers, not fusion, drive demand now

Every signed helium-3 deal is for cooling quantum computers. No fusion reactor can use helium-3 yet.

One company holds most of the deals

Interlune has all of the publicly known helium-3 purchase contracts. If its tests fail, buyers have no Moon supply to fall back on.

Two separate chains

China's program builds its own landers with state companies. The U.S. chain runs on private companies paid by NASA. The two are not connected.

No red flags found, yet

None of the companies checked had sanctions or forced-labor warnings in TradeVerifyd. Their suppliers were not all checked.

Limits. Trade records only show goods that crossed a border. They miss deals inside one country, and the products listed are customs codes, not exact parts.
