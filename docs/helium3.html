Why helium-3?

The Sun has been filling the Moon with helium-3 for billions of years

Helium-3 is a rare, light form of helium. Earth has almost none. The Moon has much more, because nothing protects it from the Sun's "solar wind."

  1. The Sun blows a wind. It sends out a constant stream of tiny charged particles, called the solar wind. A very small share of it is helium-3.
  2. Earth is shielded. Earth's magnetic field and thick air keep the solar wind from reaching the ground. So almost no helium-3 lands here.
  3. The Moon is not. It has no global magnetic field and almost no air. The solar wind hits its surface directly, except for a few days each month when it passes behind Earth.
  4. Helium-3 gets stuck. The particles bury themselves a tiny distance into soil grains, about 26 nanometers deep. A titanium mineral called ilmenite holds on to them best.
  5. Heat lets it out. Heating the soil to around 700 °C releases the trapped gas, along with hydrogen, water and other helium.

Why some places have more

The amount of helium-3 depends on three things:

  • Titanium. Ilmenite, a titanium-iron mineral, holds helium much better than other minerals. Dark lava plains rich in titanium have more.
  • Time in the sun. Soil that has sat at the surface longer has caught more wind.
  • How much wind arrives. The far side gets more, because the near side spends a few days each month shielded behind Earth.

That is why the U.S. Geological Survey model puts the richest soil in the titanium-rich plains of the near side and the lava plains of the far side.

Earth vs. the Moon

< 10 kg

All the helium-3 reserves on Earth, about 5 parts per trillion. That is why it is so expensive.

4–8 ppb

Careful estimate of helium-3 in Moon soil: 3.7 parts per billion in the highlands, 7.8 in the lava plains. Some Apollo soils reached about 20 ppb.

11.8 ppb

Average helium-3 measured in Neil Armstrong's Apollo 11 soil sample from the Sea of Tranquility.

1–2.5 million t

Estimates of all the helium-3 on the Moon. Chinese scientists say "at least 1 million tonnes." A 2007 study estimated about 2.5 million. Nobody knows how much can really be collected.

The catch: you have to dig a lot of dirt

To get 1 kg of helium-3, you would need to process 100,000 to 1,000,000 tonnes of Moon soil.

At that speed, one machine digging nonstop would need about 1,000 to 10,000 hours, roughly 6 weeks to 14 months, to gather 1 kg. That is why companies plan fleets of machines.

The calculation: 100,000 t ÷ 100 t per hour = 1,000 hours; 1,000,000 t ÷ 100 t per hour = 10,000 hours.

Why anyone would pay for it

What helium-3 is used for

  • Quantum computers. Their chips must be cooled to nearly absolute zero. The refrigerators that do this need helium-3.
  • Neutron detectors. Used to detect nuclear material.
  • Medical imaging. Used in some kinds of body scans.
  • Fusion power, someday. Helium-3 could fuel a cleaner kind of fusion, but no reactor can do it yet.
